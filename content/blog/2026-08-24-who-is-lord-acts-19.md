<p>Paul spent roughly three years in and around Ephesus &mdash; two of them teaching daily in the hall of Tyrannus, and by his own later account, ministering night and day. The result, Acts tells us, is that all the residents of Asia heard the word of the Lord. Ephesus was a hub of commerce, so people passed through, did business, and carried what they heard back home. It was also a hub of religion. The temple of Artemis there was one of the seven wonders of the ancient world, and the city's association with magic was so strong that spells and incantations were commonly called Ephesian words.</p><p>So when Paul walked in preaching Jesus, he wasn't arguing people into believing in the supernatural. They already did. Some of them wanted the spiritual things of Jesus without making Him Lord. And as this Sunday's message put it, that is still a problem today. There is a difference between believing that Jesus exists and making Him Lord of your life. The question the message asked early and never let go of: we all want more of Jesus, but do we want to give Jesus more of us?</p><h2>When Jesus becomes Lord, imitation gets exposed</h2><p>Acts 19 introduces some traveling Jewish exorcists &mdash; a real phenomenon in the ancient world; the first-century historian Josephus records a Jewish man named Eliezer performing an exorcism in front of a Roman ruler using a ring and a root. Seven sons of a Jewish high priest named Sceva had watched Paul. They had seen the sick healed and evil spirits leave, and they concluded the power must be in the words. So they tried them.</p><p>Their phrasing gives away everything: I adjure you by the Jesus whom Paul proclaims. Not the Jesus I know. Not the Jesus who is Lord of my life. The evil spirit answered, Jesus I know, and Paul I recognize, but who are you? They fled that house naked and wounded.</p><p>They mistook relationship for technique. Sunday's illustration was Milli Vanilli &mdash; the act that looked the part, dressed the part, danced the part, sold millions of records and won a Grammy for Best New Artist, and then ran off stage the moment the track skipped live and exposed that the voices were never theirs. The application was uncomfortably direct: you can learn how Christians talk, learn to raise your hands, learn to say amen, grow up in church knowing all the songs and stories, and still be a fake. Imitation works until something demands the real thing.</p><p>The difference between Paul and those seven men was posture. Paul was not standing over Jesus trying to use His authority; Paul was standing under Jesus, surrendered to it. Jesus is not a power to be mastered. He's a Lord to be known.</p><h2>When Jesus becomes Lord, compromise gets burned</h2><p>Verse 18 contains a phrase worth slowing down for: also many of those who were now believers came confessing and divulging their practices. These were not first-time hearers. They already believed &mdash; and some were still holding onto pieces of the old life. There are things that can exist in your life when you believe in Jesus that cannot exist when Jesus becomes Lord of your life.</p><p>The first sign that Jesus is truly becoming Lord is that we stop hiding sin. Sunday named the four dodges: we hide it, we defend it, we minimize it, or we rename it so it no longer carries its biblical name while remaining exactly the same thing. Then came the spoiled milk. Nobody opens the fridge, smells that, and pours it over a bowl of Frosted Flakes anyway. We know it's harmful. And yet so many of us do that exact thing spiritually.</p><p>The Ephesians did the math differently. They gathered their magic books &mdash; things they had invested in, things that had been part of their lives for years &mdash; and burned them in the sight of all. The value came to fifty thousand pieces of silver, roughly twelve million dollars. The message paired that with a story from years ago: a friend who had gone from staunch atheism into Christ carried a box of occult books into the church office, walked them across the parking lot into the dumpster, and stood there while prayer was made over him. Within a minute the garbage truck pulled in and hauled them away, and joy and peace flooded his soul.</p><p>Both arrived at the same conclusion: Jesus is worth more than anything this world could offer. Which is why the message reframed the question. Stop asking how much surrender will cost, and start asking what compromise will cost. Bitterness, secret sin, constant anger, unforgiveness &mdash; these bill you in peace and integrity, potentially in your marriage and your children, potentially in your calling, and always in your relationship with God. Sometimes keeping sin costs you far more than burning it ever will.</p><h2>When Jesus becomes Lord, idols get threatened</h2><p>The chapter's final movement isn't about a demon or a bonfire. It's about a business. Demetrius, a silversmith who made silver shrines for the worship of Artemis, gathered the craftsmen with a candid opening line: Men, you know that from this business we have our wealth. Conversions were cutting into sales. The gospel had reached his wallet.</p><p>It's one thing to tolerate Jesus when He stays in the church building or in a private life. It's another when Jesus starts changing the economy &mdash; when people stop buying what they were in bondage to, when they stop supporting the things that once controlled them. Idols don't fear criticism nearly as much as they fear being replaced.</p><p>The message brought this home to north Idaho. In Wallace, in the Silver Valley, prostitution operated openly from as early as the mid-1880s until it was largely shut down in 1988, with some operation continuing secretly until 1991 &mdash; and the town still holds the Molly B. Dam festival in honor of Maggie Hall. Heather Branstetter, who wrote the book documenting it, records that many pastors and priests accepted the arrangement because they received funds, and that clergy who spoke openly against it were often driven out of town. Everyone knew. It wasn't hidden. But it brought in money. Sometimes an idol or sin survives not because everybody believes it's right, but because people are benefiting from keeping it.</p><p>Note that Paul, writing about this same season, says in 1 Corinthians 16 that a wide door for effective work has opened to him and there are many adversaries &mdash; both in the same breath. Sometimes opposition is not evidence that God is failing.</p><h2>The one question underneath the whole chapter</h2><p>A mob eventually filled a theater archaeologists say seated 22,000 people, and for nearly two hours they chanted, Great is Artemis of the Ephesians. Artemis held people captive; Jesus was setting them free. Artemis had a temple; Jesus was transforming hearts. Artemis had money and a crowd; Jesus was prevailing.</p><p>Underneath all of it sits one question: who is Lord? The message ended not with do you believe, and not even with do you love Jesus, but with a diagnostic anyone can run in about ten seconds: what in my life would make me angry if Jesus asked me to give it up? If something surfaced, that's an idol &mdash; not because Jesus wants to take good things away, but because the moment we say Jesus can have everything here but not this, we've built a throne for a second occupant. And He will not share it.</p><p>Lordship means giving Jesus access to everything: the calendar, the wallet, the relationships, the future, the comfort, the dreams. Maybe for you, today is the day to put something in the fire.
