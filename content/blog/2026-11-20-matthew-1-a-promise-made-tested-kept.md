<p>Most of us skip the genealogies. The long lists of names that are hard to pronounce feel like something to get through before the story starts. But on Sunday, as The River Church began a trip through the Gospel of Matthew that will carry us through Christmas and into the new year, we slowed down and preached one. It turns out Matthew 1:1-17 is one of the most hopeful passages in the Bible.</p><p>The message started with a confession. Our pastor's son Israel, who just turned 16, asked his dad to teach him how to shave. Tracy put the shaving kit right next to the bed. Then came about three months of Saturdays, each one passing without the lesson. He eventually kept his word, but it took a while. We can all relate. We mean well and still don't follow through. That raises the question this chapter answers: does God ever fail to follow through?</p><h2>A Promise Made</h2><p>Matthew introduces Jesus with two names: the son of David, the son of Abraham. In Genesis 12, God called Abram to leave his country and promised that through him all the families of the earth would be blessed. In 2 Samuel 7, God promised David that his house, his kingdom and his throne would be established forever. Solomon was great, but his reign ended. Every king after him died. The promise to David pointed past all of them to a King whose reign would never end.</p><p>Abraham tells us that Jesus brings the blessing. David tells us that Jesus wears the crown. Neither man lived to see it, but God saw the fulfillment before Abraham could even see the next step.</p><p>Matthew also doesn't clean up the family tree. Rahab, a prostitute from Jericho who acted in faith, is there. So is Ruth, a Moabite widow who had lost everything and should never have been part of the Messiah's line. Most families have stories they don't tell at Thanksgiving. Matthew includes the embarrassing parts on purpose, because this isn't just a family tree. This is a record of God being faithful and keeping his word to unfaithful people.</p><h2>A Promise Tested</h2><p>Verse 6 says David was the father of Solomon by the wife of Uriah. Any Jewish reader would catch the reference right away: David took Bathsheba and sent Uriah to the front lines to die. David was the king, but not the perfect king. After him the line runs through revivals under Hezekiah and Josiah and deep wickedness under Ahaz and Manasseh. The kingdom divided after Solomon died around 931 BC. The north fell to Assyria around 722 BC. Around 597 BC King Jeconiah was taken to Babylon, and in 586 BC Jerusalem was destroyed and the temple burned.</p><p>If you had stood in those ruins, you might have asked where God's promise had gone. Sin had real consequences, and the exile was God's judgment on persistent unfaithfulness. But God could discipline His people without abandoning His promises. The collapse of the kingdom could not cancel the covenant of God. At the end of 2 Kings, Jeconiah is released from prison and honored in Babylon. The throne wasn't restored yet, but David's line had not disappeared.</p><p>Last Christmas Eve our church held a bonfire after the service. The fire was put out, and it was pouring rain, yet later that night the fire department called because it had started up again. One ember had kept glowing under the charred wood. Sometimes God's faithfulness looks less like a blazing fire and more like an ember that he refuses to let go out.</p><h2>A Promise Kept</h2><p>All through the list, the pattern repeats: this man was the father of that man. Then verse 16 breaks it. Jacob was the father of Joseph, the husband of Mary, of whom Jesus was born. Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the Virgin Mary. He isn't just another child in the line. He is the promised Messiah, the blessing promised to Abraham and the King promised to David, finally under one name. The family tree has a destination, the destination is Jesus.</p><h2>What This Means While You Wait</h2><p>You may be holding on to something God has spoken over your life while everything around you seems to be falling apart. You cannot measure God's faithfulness by how favourable your circumstances look. God's promises aren't less certain because His timetable is longer than ours. When the wait feels long, look to Jesus. When the world seems to be falling apart, look to Jesus. When you cannot see what God is doing, you can still trust who God is.</p><p>Join us this Sunday in Post Falls as we keep going through Matthew 1 this Christmas season, or watch the full message at <a href="https://theriverag.church">theriverag.church</a>.</p>
