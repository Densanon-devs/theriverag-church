<p>A lot of us in north Idaho share the same memory. Silver Lake Bible Camp. Acquire the Fire. The band playing live worship, three hundred kids rushing the stage, hands stretched out at the edge of the altar. Somewhere in that week there was a night we called cry night, and we meant every word of what we prayed there. Then camp ended, and we went home to the same people, the same jokes, the same sin.</p><p>That gap is where this Sunday's message in Acts 20&ndash;22 began. As we were reminded, <em>Dying for Jesus is easier in theory than it is to live for Jesus in public.</em> Camp taught many of us that God was personal &mdash; that He would save, forgive, and bless. What we were slower to learn is that He also intends to use us. The diagnosis was blunt: <em>The Lord's people seem more interested in saving their lives than laying them down for his sake.</em></p><h2>A Prophet, a Belt, and a Room Full of Tears</h2><p>Paul is not looking for a way out of Jerusalem. Acts 21 reads like a shipping manifest &mdash; Cos, Rhodes, Patara, past Cyprus, Tyre, Ptolemais, Caesarea &mdash; and every port is another chance to turn around. He doesn't. At Tyre the ship unloads cargo for a week, long enough for the Lord's Day, long enough for the Spirit to speak, and the whole church walks him out of the city to weep on the beach. <em>That's what obedience looks like when the people who love you are crying on the shore.</em></p><p>In Caesarea he stays in the house of Philip &mdash; one of the seven from Acts 6, the man who preached in Samaria and to the Ethiopian, whose four unmarried daughters prophesied. Into that house walks Agabus, the same prophet who correctly foretold the famine in Acts 11. He takes Paul's belt, binds his own hands and feet, and says this is how the Jews at Jerusalem will bind the man who owns this belt.</p><p>Read it carefully. Agabus described the binding. He never said don't go. That is a preview of what's coming, not a veto on the mission. The people add the don't go, and they add it out of love. Paul's answer isn't a scolding and it isn't a death wish: he is ready not only to be imprisoned but to die in Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus. Eventually the room stops arguing and says, let the will of the Lord be done &mdash; not resignation, but people admitting they can't see the whole picture and trusting God anyway.</p><p>The road is not original to Paul. In Luke 9, when the days drew near for Him to be taken up, Jesus set His face to go to Jerusalem, told the twelve exactly what would happen, and refused to be talked off the road. Which is why the sharpest application landed here: <em>Warning is a gift. Disobedience dressed as caution is not.</em></p><h2>The Bill He Didn't Owe</h2><p>Paul arrives to a glad welcome and a waiting rumor: that he's been telling Jews among the Gentiles to forsake Moses. It isn't true &mdash; he fought at the Jerusalem council so Gentiles wouldn't be yoked to the law, and in the very next chapter he circumcised Timothy so Jews would listen to him. But a rumor doesn't have to be true to work.</p><p>So James proposes something public and expensive: four men are under a Nazarite vow; take them, purify yourself with them, and pay their expenses so they can shave their heads. In other words, do something the slanderer cannot explain. Paul does it. No argument is recorded.</p><p>This is not Paul pretending the law saves. This is the man who wrote Galatians, who refused to yield to false brothers even for a moment and opposed Peter to his face when the gospel was at stake. Here the gospel isn't at stake &mdash; his reputation is. <em>We don't move the gospel to keep the peace, but we don't use our freedom to slam the door on a brother.</em> The gospel is an iron pillar. Our culture, our custom, our preference, even our reputation are reeds. They can bend. And note what didn't happen: the Asian Jews inflame the city anyway. Obedience is not justified by whether the rumor dies.</p><h2>Three Sentences on the Barracks Steps</h2><p>Paul is dragged, beaten, nearly killed, and the temple gates slam shut &mdash; the last time Luke mentions the temple in Acts. Claudius Lysias arrives and binds him with two chains, and Agabus's word comes true. The mob screams the same thing that was screamed at Jesus: away with him.</p><p>And then, bruised and chained on the steps of the Antonia barracks, Paul asks his captor &mdash; who thought he was the Egyptian assassin who led four thousand into the wilderness &mdash; for permission to speak. He gets it, raises his hand, and addresses the crowd in Hebrew. Greek would have gotten him the microphone; their own language got him the silence.</p><p>Then watch what he leaves out. No systematic theology. No council letter. He tells a Jewish story to a Jewish crowd: I was trained at the feet of Gamaliel, as zealous as you are today, persecuting this way to the death. Then Damascus, the light, the voice: I am Jesus of Nazareth whom you are persecuting. Then Ananias, a devout man according to the law, and sight restored, and a commission to be a witness. They track with him until one word &mdash; Gentiles &mdash; and the room detonates. <em>The point of your testimony is three sentences: who I was, who he is, and what he's done and doing.</em></p><h2>The Altar and the Ordinary Monday</h2><p>Two pictures were held up at the end. The first is the altar, hands raised, tears streaming, and the sentence isn't fake &mdash; you mean it. The second is your ordinary day: the hard obedience with your name on it, the brother who will only hear you if you go to where he is, the person in front of you who doesn't need your whole story, just Jesus.</p><p>Do hard things without love and you get stubbornness. Become all things without a spine and you get people pleasing. And <em>talking about Jesus without a crucified life becomes words with no wounds behind them.</em> Revelation 12:11 holds the pieces together &mdash; the blood of the Lamb first, the life laid down next, and the word comes out of both.</p><p>So here is the question the message ended on: <em>You say that you're willing to die for Jesus. Are you willing to live for it?</em> Name the hard thing this week, and do it. Watch or listen to the full message at <a href="https://theriverag.church">theriverag.church</a>.</p>
