When Your Battery Is Low: Discouragement and Acts 18

Everyone knows the notification. Ten percent. Then five. Then that little red sliver, and the phone starts changing its own behavior — dimming, closing things down, conserving. Sunday's message in Acts 18 opened there, with the story of a coworker named Cody who is, in the speaker's words, incapable of charging his cell phone, and the extra cables and battery packs that got carried around on his behalf.

The observation underneath the joke is what stuck: when the battery gets low, important things change. We stop responding. We stop answering. We don't connect with the phone because we don't want to lose the phone. And that, it turns out, is a fairly precise description of what discouraged people do with God and with each other.

Paul Walked Into Corinth on Empty

Acts 18 opens with Paul leaving Athens for Corinth. Athens had not been a disaster — some believed, some were converted — but it was not the resounding movement seen in Ephesus or what was coming in Corinth. It was, as the message put it, kind of discouraging. Paul himself seems to reflect on the shift in 1 Corinthians 2, where he writes that he did not come proclaiming the testimony of God with lofty speech or wisdom, but in weakness and in fear and much trembling.

And Corinth was no soft landing. Athens was an old city of perhaps ten to twenty thousand. Corinth had 200,000 Roman citizens — and Rome only counted citizens. Estimates put the real population somewhere near 700,000 to 800,000, roughly 500,000 of them slaves. Behind the city rose the Acrocorinth, a tall hill crowned with a fortress where the rulers lived, and a temple of Aphrodite with a thousand temple prostitutes. The culture was so notorious that people used it as a verb: being Corinthianized.

Then the synagogue rejected him. Paul shook out his garments and said your blood be on your own heads. Past rejection, present atmosphere, personal exhaustion — the message named discouragement as something that comes in layers. And the definition offered was worth writing down: Discouragement is not unbelief. It is the honest weight of a servant who has given everything and wonders if it still matters.

The First Cure: Partners Already in Place

Before Paul arrived, God had already moved the pieces. Claudius had expelled all the Jews from Rome, and that imperial edict landed a tentmaking couple, Priscilla and Aquila, right where Paul would need them. He shared their trade, stayed in their home, and worked with his hands.

The message drew attention to a detail easily missed: across every appearance of Priscilla and Aquila in the New Testament, it is never recorded that Paul ministered to them. They ministered to him. Then Silas and Timothy arrived from Macedonia, and the isolation ended. Crispus, the ruler of the synagogue, believed with his whole household — and fruit appeared right next door to the place of rejection, at the house of Titus Justus.

The application was direct. Fellowship is not a luxury; the Bible does not say you must attend church to be saved, but it does say not to neglect that fellowship we have with one another. And more pointedly: Vulnerability in the body is not weakness. It is the doorway through which the Lord's comfort arises. One of the things that breaks discouragement is simply receiving ministry — which pride makes remarkably difficult. Note also that Luke names Priscilla alongside her husband, a real distinction in a culture where women were often regarded as their husbands' property, and that she will later help instruct Apollos.

The Second Cure: A Word in the Night

Then God speaks. Do not be afraid, but go on speaking and do not be silent, for I am with you and no one will attack you to harm you, for I have many in this city who are my people. Paul stayed a year and six months.

Two clarifications came with that promise. First, no one will attack you to harm you is not a force field — Paul was among the most stoned men in the New Testament, beaten so severely his attackers assumed he was dead, and he got back up and walked back in to preach. The message drew the line clearly: The physical blows may still come, but they can't touch the security of our soul when it is held by Christ. Second, I have many in this city who are my people does not mean a church already existed in Corinth. It means God had prepared hearts. The harvest was ready; Paul's job was to go gather it.

The Third Cure: Protection No One Engineered

The chapter closes with a courtroom scene. The Jews bring Paul before the tribunal, and before he can speak, the proconsul Gallio dismisses the entire case: I refuse to be the judge of these things. Gallio's tenure in Achaia is corroborated by the Delphi inscription, dated to roughly AD 51 or 52 — a small anchor confirming the timeline of Paul's stay.

What looks like bureaucratic indifference was strategic cover. In the Roman world a religion needed official recognition to spread legally, and Gallio's refusal effectively classified the Christian movement under Judaism — leaving the third missionary journey legally free to proceed. As the message put it: He uses pagan officials, political appointments, and even the laziness of powerful people to shield his work and his workers.

The chapter ends with Paul cutting his hair at Cenchreae, likely completing a Nazirite vow — a set-apart season closed with gratitude after a dark valley. Then he sails on, strengthening the disciples, because as the closing made plain, the goal was never converts. The goal is rooted, encouraged, word-taught believers that could go and make disciples of their own.

Remember the battery pack. When the charge runs low, everything in us says shut down and back away. The Lord says the opposite: stay connected and keep strengthening.
